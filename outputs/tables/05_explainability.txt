==============================================================================
3. FAIRNESS: SHOULD AGE AND GENDER BE IN THIS MODEL AT ALL?
==============================================================================

  This is a UK financial-services retention decision, so the question is
  legal before it is technical.

  The argument FOR using them: a retention offer is a benefit, not a
  denial of service. Withholding credit by protected characteristic is
  the classic harm; here we are deciding who gets offered a discount.

  The argument AGAINST, which I find stronger:
    - Sex, age and marital status are protected characteristics under
      the Equality Act 2010. Allocating commercial benefit by them
      invites a discrimination challenge whatever the intent.
    - 'The model found it predictive' is not a defence anyone wants to
      make to the FCA under Consumer Duty, which asks whether outcomes
      are fair across customer groups.
    - The reputational asymmetry is brutal: 'bank offers worse retention
      deals to women' is a headline; a 0.002 PR-AUC gain is not.

  How the CURRENT model behaves per group (test set, already scored):

  by gender:
    group           n    churn    PR-AUC   ROC-AUC  targeted@250
    F           1,060   17.26%    0.2970    0.6591        15.6%
    M             966   14.70%    0.2693    0.6677         8.8%

  by age_band:
    group           n    churn    PR-AUC   ROC-AUC  targeted@250
    <35           187   14.44%    0.3591    0.7118        11.8%
    35-44         751   17.31%    0.3149    0.6798        11.1%
    45-54         804   15.92%    0.2600    0.6255        11.8%
    55+           284   14.08%    0.2988    0.7203        17.6%

  DISPARITY: targeting rate against underlying risk

  A group that churns more SHOULD be targeted more. The question is
  whether targeting is proportionate to risk. The ratio below is
  (share targeted) / (actual churn rate): equal values across groups
  means proportionate treatment, whatever the underlying rates.

  by gender:
    group         churn  targeted    ratio
    F            17.26%    15.57%     0.90
    M            14.70%     8.80%     0.60
    -> 'F' is targeted 1.51x as hard per unit of
       actual risk as 'M'

  by age_band:
    group         churn  targeted    ratio
    <35          14.44%    11.76%     0.81
    35-44        17.31%    11.05%     0.64
    45-54        15.92%    11.82%     0.74
    55+          14.08%    17.61%     1.25
    -> '55+' is targeted 1.96x as hard per unit of
       actual risk as '35-44'

  Stated plainly for gender: women churn 1.17x as often as
  men in this test set, but are targeted 1.77x as often.
  The targeting gap is materially wider than the risk gap, which is
  what disparate impact looks like in a targeting model. It is
  visible here only because the check was run -- no metric in stage 4
  would have surfaced it.

4. WHAT DOES REMOVING AGE AND GENDER COST?
==============================================================================

  Measured by repeated CV on the training set. Not on test: stage 4
  spent the one permitted test pass, and a second would undermine the
  number reported there.

  with age + gender    : PR-AUC 0.2425
  without age + gender : PR-AUC 0.2427
  cost of removal      : -0.0002 PR-AUC
  paired fold interval : [-0.0246, +0.0211]
  keeping them wins in 48% of 25 folds

  The paired interval spans zero: removing them costs nothing the
  data can distinguish from noise.

==============================================================================
5. DOES REMOVING THEM ACTUALLY REMOVE THE INFORMATION?
==============================================================================

  Dropping a column does not delete what it encoded if other features
  predict it. Testing that directly: can the remaining SAFE features
  recover gender and age band?

  predicting gender     from the other features: ROC-AUC 0.9552
  predicting age 55+    from the other features: ROC-AUC 0.9102

  ROC-AUC 0.9552. Gender survives its own deletion almost
  intact. Dropping the column is very nearly cosmetic.

  WHICH FEATURE IS DOING IT:

    feature                     ROC-AUC for gender
    income_category                         0.9488
    income_category_ord                     0.9275
    credit_limit                            0.7542
    income_is_unknown                       0.4025

  income_category by gender (% of each gender in each band):

    band                     F       M
    $120K +                0.0    15.9
    $40K - $60K           18.9    16.7
    $60K - $80K            0.0    28.8
    $80K - $120K           0.0    32.3
    Less than $40K        60.6     5.4
    Unknown               20.5     1.0

  Read the zeros. In this dataset there is not one woman in the
  $60K-$80K, $80K-$120K or $120K+ bands, while 60.6% of women sit in
  'Less than $40K' against 5.4% of men. Income band above $60K
  therefore implies male with certainty, which is why a model
  recovers gender at ROC-AUC 0.96 without ever seeing it.

  Credit limit carries it too: the training-set mean is $5,123 for women against $12,907 for men.

  (A distribution this clean is almost certainly an artefact of a
  teaching dataset rather than a real portfolio. It is still the
  perfect illustration of the problem, and the check is exactly the
  one to run on real data.)

  WHAT WOULD IT TAKE TO ACTUALLY REMOVE GENDER?

    also dropping income_category, credit_limit, income_category_ord, income_is_unknown
    gender recovery falls from 0.9552 to 0.5116
    model PR-AUC falls from 0.2427 to 0.2097, a cost of 0.0329
    paired fold interval [+0.0058, +0.0579]

==============================================================================
6. RECOMMENDATION
==============================================================================

  1. REMOVE age and gender as model inputs.
     Measured cost: -0.0002 PR-AUC, paired interval
     [-0.0246, +0.0211] -- indistinguishable from
     zero. There is no performance argument for keeping them, so the
     legal and reputational exposure is unpaid-for risk.

  2. BUT DO NOT CLAIM THE MODEL IS THEREFORE GENDER-BLIND.
     Gender is still recoverable at ROC-AUC 0.9552 from the
     remaining features, overwhelmingly through income_category.
     A fairness claim based on 'we do not use gender' would be
     false, and falsifiable by anyone who runs the check above.

  3. The real choice is between two honest positions:

     (a) Keep income and credit limit, accept that the model
         encodes gender, and manage it by monitoring OUTCOMES by
         group rather than by policing inputs.
     (b) Drop income and credit limit too. Recovery falls to
         0.5116 -- genuinely gender-blind -- at a cost
         of 0.0329 PR-AUC, which is 40% of
         the model's entire margin over random.

     I recommend (a), for a specific reason: income and credit limit
     are legitimate, causally-relevant predictors of retention in a
     credit product. Removing genuine economic signal to break a
     statistical association is the kind of fix that degrades the
     model without helping anyone. Fairness here has to be measured
     on who gets the offer, not on which columns were fed in.

  4. Therefore the fairness control is an OUTCOME control:
     - report targeting rate and offer acceptance by gender and age
       band every campaign cycle, not once at build time
     - set a tolerance in advance for divergence between a group's
       targeting rate and its actual churn rate
     - escalate to a human when it breaches, rather than silently
       rebalancing the model

  What none of this fixes: removing inputs does not guarantee equal
  outcomes, and this dataset has no offer-response data, so whether the
  campaign itself lands differently across groups is untestable here.
  That is a question for the stage 7 experiment.
