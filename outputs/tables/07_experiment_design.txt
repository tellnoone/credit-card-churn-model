==============================================================================
STAGE 7: EXPERIMENT DESIGN FOR THE RETENTION OFFER
==============================================================================

  Predicting churn is not the same as predicting who an offer can save.
  Everything in stage 6 assumed a save rate of 25% because the data
  cannot measure one. This is how you would measure it.

==============================================================================
1. WHO IS IN THE EXPERIMENT
==============================================================================

  Not the whole book. The experiment runs on the segment a campaign
  would actually target, because that is the population the save rate
  needs to be true for. A save rate measured on low-risk customers
  would not transfer.

  Defining the segment by model score decile:

  segment                 n (test)   churn rate  scaled to book
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  top 5%                       101       39.6%             506
  top 10%                      202       34.7%           1,012
  top 20%                      405       30.9%           2,025
  top 30%                      607       26.9%           3,038
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  whole book                 2,026       16.0%          10,127

  CHOSEN: the top 20% by churn score.

  Why not the top 5%, where risk is highest? Because at 506 customers
  on the full book there are not enough of them to power the test in a
  reasonable window, as section 3 shows. The top 20% trades a slightly
  lower baseline churn rate for enough customers to get an answer.

  This is the real tension in retention experiments: the segment where
  the offer matters most is the segment with fewest people in it.

==============================================================================
2. DESIGN
==============================================================================

  Arms                 : 50/50 randomised split within the segment
    - TREATMENT        : receives the retention offer
    - CONTROL          : receives nothing, and is not contacted at all

  Randomisation is at CUSTOMER level, assigned from a hash of the
  customer id so assignment is stable if the campaign re-runs, and
  independent of anything the model saw.

  PRIMARY METRIC: churn within 90 days of assignment.

    One metric, fixed in advance. 90 days is long enough for a
    retention effect to appear and short enough to decide inside a
    planning cycle. Measured identically in both arms, including for
    customers who never open the offer -- this is intention-to-treat.

    Analysing only customers who ENGAGED with the offer would be the
    single easiest way to fake a positive result here, because
    engagement is itself a symptom of not churning.

  SECONDARY (monitored, not decisive):
    - offer redemption rate        : did anyone want it
    - spend in the 90-day window   : did retention cost engagement
    - churn at 180 days            : did we delay rather than prevent

  GUARDRAIL: total 90-day margin per customer, treatment vs control.
    A retained customer who costs more in offer than they generate is
    not a win, and the primary metric cannot see that.

==============================================================================
3. HOW BIG, AND FOR HOW LONG
==============================================================================

  Baseline churn in the top 20% segment: 30.9%

  The MDE should not be plucked from the air. The commercially
  meaningful threshold is the save rate at which the campaign breaks
  even, because below it the offer destroys value however significant
  the result is:

    break-even save rate = offer_cost / (value x baseline churn)
                         = 40 / (300 x 0.309)
                         = 43.2%

  So the MDE is a save rate of 43.2%.

  That number is the finding, and it is not a comfortable one. A
  43% save rate means retaining nearly half of everyone who
  would otherwise have left. Published retention effects sit closer to
  10-30%. At a GBP 40 offer against GBP 300 of annual margin, the offer
  has to clear a bar that the literature suggests is not reachable.

  This is stage 6's conclusion arriving from a different direction. The
  problem is not that the model is weak; it is that the offer economics
  do not work. Before running any experiment, the cheaper move is to
  change those economics -- see the alternative below.

  Churn under the offer at the MDE : 17.5%
  Absolute effect to detect        : 13.3 percentage points
  alpha 0.05, power 80%, two-sided

  REQUIRED SAMPLE: 161 per arm, 322 total

  Available in the segment on a 10,127-customer book: 2,025
  -> fits in one pass. Note this is only because the effect being
     looked for is implausibly large; large effects are cheap to
     detect. A small required sample is not good news here.

==============================================================================
3b. THE EXPERIMENT WORTH ACTUALLY RUNNING
==============================================================================

  Rather than test for an effect nobody expects, change the economics
  first so that a plausible save rate pays, then test for that.

  Cut the offer cost to GBP 15 (a points nudge rather than a
  fee waiver) and the break-even save rate falls to 16.2%.
  That is inside the range retention offers actually achieve.

  Powering for a 20% save rate instead:
    churn under offer   : 24.7%
    absolute effect     : 6.2 pp
    REQUIRED SAMPLE     : 826 per arm, 1,651 total
    available in segment: 2,025
    -> fits in one pass

  This is the design I would actually propose: a cheaper offer, tested
  for an effect size someone might plausibly observe, on a segment big
  enough to answer the question.

==============================================================================
3c. WHAT A SMALLER TEST COULD SEE
==============================================================================

  What a smaller test could see, if the business will not wait:

       n per arm    detectable save rate   power at MDE
------------------------------------------------------------------------------
             250                  35.3%            94%
             500                  25.4%           100%
           1,000                  18.2%           100%
           2,000                  13.0%           100%
           5,000                   8.3%           100%
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Read the top row: a 250-per-arm test can only detect a save rate
  around 35%. If the true save rate is the 20% that
  retention offers plausibly achieve, that test returns 'no significant
  effect' most of the time and the offer gets killed for the wrong
  reason. An underpowered experiment is worse than none, because it
  produces a confident answer.

==============================================================================
4. ANALYSIS AND STOPPING RULE
==============================================================================

  Fixed horizon. The test runs to its full sample and is analysed once.

  No peeking at the primary metric. Checking repeatedly and stopping at
  the first significant result inflates the false-positive rate well
  above the nominal 5%. If interim looks are required for governance,
  they must be declared in advance and use an alpha-spending boundary
  (O'Brien-Fleming), which prices the option to stop early rather than
  pretending it is free.

  Analysis: two-proportion test on 90-day churn, intention-to-treat,
  reporting the absolute difference with a 95% confidence interval. The
  interval is the output, not the p-value -- the business needs to know
  the plausible range of the save rate, not whether it clears a line.

  DECISION RULE, fixed now:
    - CI lower bound above a 43% save rate -> roll out
    - CI spans 43% -> inconclusive; extend or redesign the offer
    - CI entirely below it -> stop. The offer does not pay, whatever
      its p-value

  VALIDITY CHECKS before any outcome is read: sample ratio mismatch
  against the intended 50/50, and balance on pre-assignment covariates.
  A failed SRM invalidates the comparison regardless of the result.

==============================================================================
5. WHY UPLIFT MODELLING IS THE NEXT STEP, NOT THIS ONE
==============================================================================

  This project ranks customers by P(churn). The policy then assumes the
  highest-risk customers are the ones worth contacting. That assumption
  is wrong in a specific, well-documented way.

  Every customer falls into one of four groups:

    SURE THINGS   stay whether or not you contact them
                  -> the offer is pure cost
    LOST CAUSES   leave whether or not you contact them
                  -> the offer is pure cost
    PERSUADABLES  stay only if contacted
                  -> the offer creates all of its value here
    SLEEPING DOGS stay UNLESS contacted; the approach reminds them
                  they were considering leaving
                  -> the offer actively destroys value

  A churn model cannot tell these apart. It scores LOST CAUSES highest
  of all, because they are the most likely to churn -- and they are
  precisely the customers an offer cannot save. That is a plausible
  reason a well-built churn-targeted campaign underdelivers: the model
  works exactly as designed and still spends the budget on people whose
  minds are made up.

  An uplift (CATE) model estimates P(stay | contacted) - P(stay | not),
  which is the quantity the policy actually wants. It ranks
  PERSUADABLES first and pushes SLEEPING DOGS to the bottom.

  Why it cannot be built first: uplift models are trained on the
  outcome of a randomised experiment. They need customers who were
  contacted and customers who were not, assigned at random. That data
  does not exist until the experiment in sections 1-4 has run.

  So the order is forced, and it is worth stating plainly because it is
  the honest answer to 'why not just build an uplift model':

    1. churn model                 <- this project
    2. randomised offer experiment <- sections 1-4, measures the save rate
    3. uplift model                <- trained on the output of step 2
    4. targeting by uplift         <- what the policy should eventually use

  Step 1 is not wasted. It defines the segment worth experimenting on,
  which is what makes step 2 affordable: running the test on the whole
  book instead of the top 20% would need far more customers to
  reach the same power, because the baseline churn rate is lower.
